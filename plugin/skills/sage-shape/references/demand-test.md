# The demand test — deeper judgment calls

Load this file from `sage-shape/SKILL.md` step 3 when a piece of demand evidence is genuinely borderline and neither the table nor the cost-to-give heuristic in the main procedure resolves which side of the line it falls on. This file does not repeat the base rule — interest is not demand, behavior and money and panic-when-it-breaks are — it exists for the harder cases where that rule alone isn't quite enough to rule.

## The traps that make borderline cases hard

**Survivorship bias in who complains.** If three people out of forty complained about a missing capability, that's often read as "7.5% demand." But the thirty-seven who didn't complain aren't necessarily satisfied — some of them may have quietly stopped using the product entirely rather than complain, and churned users don't file tickets. Before treating a small complaint count as the whole picture, ask whether anyone tracked whether silence correlates with churn. A small vocal group plus an unmeasured silent departure can be a much bigger demand signal than the ticket count alone suggests — or it can mean the thirty-seven genuinely don't care and the three are an edge case. The honest answer is often "we don't know," and that uncertainty belongs in the roadmap's "why" rather than being resolved by assumption in either direction.

**The novelty effect.** Early usage numbers on anything new are inflated by curiosity — people click on new things because they're new, not because they need them. A feature that gets used heavily in its first week and drops off sharply by week three is showing you the novelty effect, not demand. If a demand claim rests on early usage data with no second data point after the novelty has had time to fade, treat it as unconfirmed and say so, rather than either accepting it wholesale or dismissing it outright.

**Circular self-validation.** "People use it because we built it" is not evidence anyone wanted it — it's evidence it was the only option in front of them at the time. This shows up often in internal tools ("everyone uses the internal wiki, so people clearly want a wiki") and in default-on features ("80% of users have this setting on" when it ships on by default). Ask what people did *before* the thing existed, and whether they had a real alternative to opting out. If using it required no decision at all, it isn't demand for that specific thing — it's default behavior.

**Sunk-cost-driven false demand.** Sometimes a stakeholder reports strong demand for a feature because they've already invested time, credibility, or budget in the idea and are motivated to see it validated. This isn't dishonesty; it's a normal bias, but it changes how much weight their read of the evidence deserves. When you're getting demand evidence secondhand from someone who proposed the project, ask for the raw signal (the actual ticket count, the actual quote) rather than their summary of it — "it's strongly requested" from an interested party is a claim to verify, not a fact to record.

**The delegation trap.** Sometimes the person you're interrogating hasn't looked at the evidence directly and answers with "the data team says there's demand" or "sales says customers want this." That's one layer removed from the actual signal. Push once for the underlying artifact — the actual ticket thread, the actual deal note, the actual survey data — rather than someone's summary of someone else's summary. If the underlying artifact genuinely isn't available, record that plainly ("demand claim is secondhand, source not verified") instead of treating a secondhand claim as if it were sourced.

## A short procedure for ruling under uncertainty

When you've applied the base table and the cost-to-give heuristic and the call still isn't clear, work through these in order rather than picking whichever answer feels right:

1. **Ask for the raw artifact.** Not "how many people want this" but "show me the actual ticket, message, or transcript." A claim that can't produce a raw artifact when asked is weaker than one that can, regardless of how confidently it was stated.
2. **Ask about the denominator.** "Three complaints" means something different out of forty users than out of forty thousand. If nobody knows the denominator, that's worth naming — a rate you can't compute isn't evidence of a rate, high or low.
3. **Ask what happened when the workaround was removed or broke, if it ever was.** Panic when something breaks is one of the strongest demand signals precisely because it's involuntary — nobody stages a complaint about a script breaking for effect. If this scenario hasn't happened, you can't use it as evidence either way; don't manufacture urgency it doesn't have.
4. **If none of the above resolves it, write the uncertainty into the roadmap rather than resolving it artificially.** "Demand: moderate, unconfirmed — three unprompted asks over two months on an unknown base; recommend instrumenting before committing further" is a completely legitimate roadmap entry. The demand test's job is to prevent false confidence, not to force every claim into a binary yes/no when the honest answer is "we don't have enough signal yet."

## More worked judgment calls

- *"A beta cohort of 20 people used the feature every day for the two weeks it was live."* — Leans toward demand, but check for the novelty effect: two weeks isn't long enough to distinguish "solved a real recurring problem" from "was new and interesting." Ask if there's a third or fourth week of data, or if the beta is still running.
- *"Our biggest customer's contract renewal explicitly lists this as a blocker."* — Demand, and about as strong a signal as exists: money, specificity, and a deadline all point the same direction. Record the account and the renewal date in the roadmap's why, not just "a customer asked."
- *"Two different teams independently built the same internal spreadsheet to solve this."* — Demand. Independent convergence on the same workaround, without either team knowing the other did it, is a stronger signal than either team's workaround alone — it rules out one team just imitating a coworker out of habit.
- *"The feature request has the most upvotes in our public roadmap tool."* — Weak, by itself. An upvote is closer to a star than a ticket — it costs almost nothing. Ask whether any of the upvoters are also customers who've complained through a costlier channel (support, sales) about the same gap; if so, that's the real evidence, and the upvote count is corroborating color at best.
- *"The team is confident because everyone they've talked to loves the idea."* — Not demand; "everyone we've talked to" is very likely a self-selected group of people predisposed to be enthusiastic (friends, existing fans, people in a pitch meeting who are being polite). Ask who was in that group and what, if anything, they were asked to do afterward — nothing beats a "loved it" that led to zero follow-on action.
- *"A regulatory deadline requires this by a specific date."* — This is not a demand signal in the interest-vs-behavior sense at all; it's a hard external constraint. Treat it as its own category in the roadmap's "why" — "regulatory requirement, deadline <date>" — rather than trying to force it into the demand framework, which assumes optional adoption.

## Calibrating the bar by context

The amount of evidence that should satisfy the demand test is not a fixed number — it scales with the cost of being wrong. A reversible, cheap, one-sprint wedge can reasonably proceed on a thinner signal than an expensive, hard-to-reverse piece of infrastructure; ask what it costs to undo this specific decision before deciding how much evidence is "enough." For a genuinely new, disruptive idea with no existing market to survey, the strongest evidence available may be first-principles reasoning plus a very small pilot rather than a large behavioral dataset — in that case, say so plainly ("evidence: reasoned hypothesis plus 3-person pilot, not yet broad behavioral signal") rather than pretending a small pilot is the same thing as validated demand at scale.
